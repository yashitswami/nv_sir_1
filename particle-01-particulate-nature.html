The Particulate Nature of Matter

🔍 Repeated subdivision of chalk ultimately reaches particles too small to observe directly.

Take a piece of chalk and break it in half. Break it again. And again. Could the division continue indefinitely? The historical question has a modern, definitive answer: no. Eventually the division reaches a fundamental unit that cannot be divided further without ceasing to be chalk. All matter is composed of such fundamental particles, far too small to be resolved even with an ordinary optical microscope.

This is the particulate nature of matter, and it is the foundational principle of this entire course. Once the particulate structure can be visualised, one can explain — rather than merely memorise — why a gas can be compressed, why a liquid conforms to the shape of its container, and why a puddle evaporates on a warm day.

Atoms, molecules and ions: naming the particle correctly

"Particle" is the general term used in the particle model, but a precise scientific description always names the specific type of particle involved. There are three fundamental categories:

Particle typeDefinitionExample
AtomThe smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical identityA single atom of iron (Fe) or helium (He)
MoleculeTwo or more atoms chemically bonded togetherA water molecule (H₂O); an oxygen molecule (O₂)
IonAn atom or molecule that has gained or lost electrons, carrying an electric chargeSodium ion (Na⁺) and chloride ion (Cl⁻) in common salt
Why this file uses "particle" as an umbrella term
Throughout this course, particle is used as the general term precisely because the fundamental unit of a substance is not always the same kind: water is composed of molecules, iron is composed of atoms, and common salt is composed of ions. Wherever a specific substance is discussed, this file names the correct particle type explicitly.

The four postulates of the particle model

The model rests on four postulates. Learn them as a set — Olympiad "assertion & reason" questions are frequently based on this list.

1 · matter is composed of minute particles (atoms, molecules or ions) 2 · particles are separated by intermolecular space 3 · particles possess continuous, random kinetic motion 4 · particles exert a mutual force of attraction

Two of these properties must never be confused: intermolecular space (the physical gap between particles) and the intermolecular force of attraction (the strength with which particles attract one another). These two properties vary independently as matter changes state, and examination questions are deliberately designed to test whether students confuse them.

The scale of a molecule
A single drop of water contains approximately one thousand billion billion (10²¹) water molecules. If each molecule were enlarged to the size of a pea, that single drop would cover an entire country to a depth of several metres. This illustrates why individual molecules can never be resolved by the unaided eye.

Kinetic Molecular Theory: The Main Postulates

The scattered observations of Section 1 can now be consolidated into a single, formally stated theory — the Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) — comprising four postulates from which the entire physical behaviour of solids, liquids and gases can be logically derived. Olympiad papers frequently quote one postulate and ask which of the four it represents, so learn the set precisely.

Postulate 1 — Composition of matter

Matter, in any physical state, is composed of exceedingly small particles — namely molecules, atoms or ions — depending on the substance under consideration.

Postulate 2 — Inter-particle space

The constituent particles of a substance are never in absolute contact with one another. They are arranged such that a finite gap — the inter-particle space — separates every particle from its neighbour. The magnitude of this space is the single most important factor distinguishing the three physical states.

Postulate 3 — Inter-particle attraction

Every particle of matter exerts a force of attraction upon every neighbouring particle. This force is maximum when the particles are close together and minimum when the particles are at a distance from one another — the exact inverse of the inter-particle space described in Postulate 2.

⚠ Do not confuse these two postulates
Inter-particle space (the physical gap) and inter-particle attraction (the pulling force) always move in opposite directions as a substance changes state: as space increases, attraction decreases, and vice versa. Examination questions are deliberately worded to test whether this inverse relationship has been understood.

Postulate 4 — Energy possessed by particles of matter

Particles of matter are never stationary; they exist in a state of continuous, random motion and therefore possess energy termed kinetic energy. On the application of heat, the kinetic energy of the particles increases, causing them to move more vigorously and more randomly.

Formal statement — commit this to memory
Matter is discontinuous, being composed of discrete particles that are (i) exceedingly minute, (ii) separated by a finite inter-particle space, (iii) held together by an inter-particle force of attraction that varies inversely with separation, and (iv) in a state of perpetual, random motion whose intensity is governed by temperature.

Evidence for the Particle Model

The Kinetic Molecular Theory is not a matter of speculation — it rests on direct experimental evidence. Three classic demonstrations are explored interactively below.

Evidence 1 — Brownian motion

In 1827, the botanist Robert Brown observed pollen grains suspended in water under a microscope and noticed that they moved in a continuous, erratic, zig-zag path — despite the water appearing perfectly still. The explanation, confirmed decades later, is that invisible, fast-moving water molecules are ceaselessly bombarding each pollen grain from random directions, and the unequal number of impacts on each side kicks the grain along an unpredictable path. Since the pollen grain is visible but the molecules causing its motion are not, Brownian motion is indirect evidence for the existence and constant motion of particles.

Evidence 2 — Dissolution and inter-particle space

When sugar is stirred into water, it appears to "disappear," yet the total volume of the resulting solution is less than the sum of the two original volumes taken separately. This can only be explained if the dissolved sugar particles are not creating new space, but instead slotting into the pre-existing inter-particle spaces of the water — direct proof that such spaces exist.

Dissolved! The sugar particles now occupy the spaces between the water particles.

Evidence 3 — The scale of particles (a dilution test)

A single crystal of a strongly coloured compound (such as potassium permanganate) can colour a surprisingly large volume of water. If that coloured solution is repeatedly diluted ten-fold, the colour astonishingly remains just visible even after enormous dilution — which is only possible if the original crystal contained an immense number of particles to begin with. This directly reinforces the scale discussed in Section 1: a single drop of water contains roughly 10²¹ molecules.
